Therefore, this experiment did not reliably identify the cache-line boundary. The result does not establish a 256-byte cache line.

For stride 1, the Karp–Flatt metric increased from 0.103121 at p=2 to 0.226652 at p=12. For stride 16, it increased from 0.045915 to 0.260962.

False sharing occurs when workers modify different words within the same cache line, generating coherence traffic. However, these measurements also include process startup and Python execution overhead. The results therefore do not isolate false sharing as the sole cause of increasing e(p).

The measured serial fraction was s=0.01561368, approximately 1.56%. Amdahl’s asymptotic ceiling was 64.0464×, and its prediction at 12 workers was 10.2411×. The observed speedup was 3.7111×, approximately 63.76% below that prediction.

This difference shows that the simple model omits important costs, including communication, scheduling and competition for processor resources.

From 5 to 6 workers, speedup increased by 0.502097. From 6 to 7 workers, it decreased by 0.419350. This represents a 183.52% reduction in marginal gain, meaning that the gain became negative; it does not mean total speedup fell by 183.52%.

The slowdown near the configured physical-core boundary is consistent with resource contention, but CPU affinity was not controlled, so it cannot be attributed conclusively to SMT.

In weak scaling, runtime increased from 573.9708 ms at p=1 to 1616.2020 ms at p=12. Weak-scaling efficiency at 12 workers was 35.51%.

The calculated Gustafson scaled speedup was 11.205139×, with α=0.072260. This is a model-based value, not a directly measured 11.2× runtime improvement. The serial stage also grows with total input size, contributing to declining weak-scaling efficiency.

The fastest tested tile size was BS=128, with a modelled footprint of 393,216 bytes (384 KiB). It achieved a 1.0934× speedup over the naive version, reducing execution time by 8.54%.

Small tiles performed poorly because additional loops and slice operations increased Python overhead. The reordered version was also slower than the naive version, demonstrating that better access locality does not automatically improve performance in this implementation.

BS=128 had the lowest observed median, but the difference from BS=96 was small relative to timing variability. It should therefore be described as the best measured configuration, rather than a conclusively established optimum.

The naive intensity estimate was 0.125 FLOP/byte, and the tiled estimate was 128/12=10.666667 FLOP/byte. All three assumed intensities exceeded the ridge point, placing them on the compute-limited side of this simplified model.

Static block scheduling showed substantial imbalance because workers handling larger indices received much more expensive work. Cyclic scheduling distributed expensive items more evenly and reduced execution time.

Among the available configurations, dynamic scheduling with C=1 was fastest, achieving a 2.2776× speedup over static block scheduling. Its λ of 1.000770 indicates almost equal worker active durations.

Although C=1 requires frequent locking, its fine-grained balancing was effective in these runs. Larger chunks reduce synchronization frequency but can leave uneven amounts of expensive work near completion. Timing changes may also reflect changing system conditions; they cannot be explained by imbalance alone.

Results for C=256 and C=1024 are missing, so the final best chunk size across the complete required sweep cannot yet be confirmed.
